# Decision log

A graded deliverable: 10–15 non-obvious decisions and why. Append as you go, while the
reason is still fresh. Keep the "what I gave up" column honest — that is the part that
reads as engineering judgement rather than justification.

| # | Decision | Why | What I gave up |
|---|----------|-----|----------------|
| 1 | Index the 3M-row CSV into sqlite rather than loading it in pandas | Thread reconstruction needs random access by `tweet_id`; an in-memory index plus the frame does not fit comfortably on a laptop, and every experiment would pay a ~60s reload | A one-time ~2 min indexing step, and SQL instead of dataframe ergonomics |
| 2 | The agent handles only the FIRST customer message of a thread | Later customer turns are responses to the brand's reply, so scoring them leaks the answer into the input | Cannot evaluate multi-turn follow-up handling |
| 3 | Cache every LLM response to disk, keyed by (model, temperature, prompt) | Free-tier quota is the binding constraint; caching makes the eval re-runnable at zero cost and makes published numbers reproducible | Cache invalidation is manual when a prompt changes |
| 4 | Choose the brand on reply substance (DM-deflection rate), not volume alone | A brand that answers "DM us" gives the drafter nothing to ground in, so reply-quality scores would measure nothing | Rules out the highest-volume brands |
| 5 | Retrieval defaults to scikit-learn TF-IDF, not sentence-transformers | `sentence-transformers` pulls ~2GB of torch, which alone would blow the 15-minute reproduction budget on a grader's machine. Gemini embeddings sit behind a config flag so the two can be compared in the report | Weaker semantic matching on paraphrases; measured, not assumed |
| 6 | Sample only after filtering, and sort by `thread_id` before sampling | Otherwise the "deterministic" seed still depends on the order `build_threads.py` happened to emit rows, so the committed sample would not be reproducible | Slightly more code than `random.sample` on the raw list |
| 7 | Built the agent for **Delta**, not the highest-volume brand | Delta's replies are self-contained single tweets carrying an actual answer, its intents are naturally bounded (rebooking, delay/cancellation, baggage, website/app, in-flight, loyalty/refund), and it has genuinely high-stakes messages that make the escalation decision worth making. AmazonHelp is bigger but heavily multilingual — an English filter would drop a large, non-random slice — and British_Airways splits replies across "1/2"/"2/2" tweets | ~4x less data than AmazonHelp, and findings may not generalise to brands with a different reply culture |
| 8 | Widened the deflection detector after reading real threads, and re-reported the numbers | The first version matched only "DM", which scored AmazonHelp at 1.2% deflection while its sampled replies were visibly full of "reach us by phone or chat here". The metric was measuring the word, not the behaviour. Brand choice was made on the corrected numbers | The corrected rate is still a keyword heuristic, so it undercounts paraphrased deflection; it is a floor, not a measurement |
| 9 | Intent taxonomy of 8 buckets, defined by reading clustered real messages rather than adopting Banking77 | Banking77's labels are banking-shaped and would have imposed categories Delta's traffic does not contain, while inventing distinctions no Delta agent makes. The clusters were used only as a reading aid — cluster 1 mixed praise, a boarding complaint and a hashtag photo, which is evidence TF-IDF groups words rather than intents | The taxonomy is fitted to one brand and would need redoing for another |
| 10 | Wrote tie-break rules BEFORE labelling and applied them to all 200 examples | Delay and rebooking overlap in most real messages ("delayed, missed my connection, can you rebook me"). Without a written rule the same message gets labelled differently on different days, and every accuracy number downstream is really measuring the labeller's consistency | The chosen rule ("label by what they ask for, not what happened") is defensible but arbitrary; a different rule would move per-class F1 |
| 11 | Revised the taxonomy AFTER labelling, when `other` came out at 42% | The first pass put 83/196 examples in `other` — not through careless labelling but because the taxonomy had no home for policy questions ("do I get priority boarding with the Delta card?"), ground-service complaints ("two hours on hold, rude staff at ATL") or chit-chat ("see you in the morning"). A 42% catch-all makes intent accuracy meaningless: a majority-class guesser scores 42% and the classes that matter operationally have too few examples to measure. Added general_question, service_complaint and social_chitchat and re-filed only the affected rows | The revised labels are not a clean held-out set: the categories were designed with these 83 examples in view, so per-class scores on them are optimistic. Stated in the report's "what is misleading" section |
| 12 | Kept the original auto/escalate decisions when re-filing intents | Those decisions were made against the same message text and re-opening them would be churn dressed as rigour, plus it would let hindsight quietly reshape the ground truth | Escalation labels reflect the labeller's first-pass judgement, including its inconsistencies |
| 13 | Did NOT tune the escalation policy after seeing the golden labels | The policy hardcodes baggage and loyalty as always-escalate; the labels say baggage escalates only 50% of the time. Editing the rules to match would fit the policy to the very set it is scored on and inflate the result while measuring nothing. The rules stay as the honest prior they were written as, the mismatch is reported as a failure mode, and recalibration on held-out data goes in "what I'd do next" | Escalation precision is worse than it could trivially be made to look |
| 14 | Kept `loyalty_refund_compensation` in the taxonomy despite only 1 golden example | It is operationally real for an airline and appears throughout the unlabelled sample; dropping it would hide a category the agent will meet in production. Per-class metrics for it are reported as unmeasurable rather than as a number | One intent has no meaningful F1, and macro-F1 is dragged down by a class with n=1 |
| 15 | Transport errors crash; only unparseable model output is scored as a failure | The first eval run died in the drafter, not the classifier — because `classify()` caught bare `Exception` and turned a retired-model 404 into `{"intent": "unparseable"}`. Every one of the 196 classifications "succeeded" while actually failing, which would have produced a real-looking intent accuracy of ~0 with no error anywhere. Silent fallbacks in an evaluation harness manufacture numbers. Now only JSON/parse errors are scored; anything else propagates | A transient network blip aborts a run instead of degrading it — mitigated by the response cache, so a rerun resumes |
| 16 | Added a live preflight call before the eval loop | A wrong model name or an exhausted quota was previously discovered several minutes into a run. One uncached call up front fails in two seconds with the provider's own message | One extra API call per run, and it deliberately bypasses the cache |
| 17 | Switched the default provider from Gemini to Groq, and made the provider swappable | Gemini retired `gemini-2.0-flash` mid-project; its named replacement allows **20 requests per day** on the free tier against the ~570 this evaluation needs, so the project was un-runnable on that path. Published rate-limit tables were stale for both models, so `scripts/check_models.py` asks the provider directly. Groq's free tier allows 1,000/day at 30 RPM with no card. Groq is called over its OpenAI-compatible HTTP endpoint using only the standard library, so no dependency is added to the grader's 15-minute setup | An open Llama model rather than a frontier one, which likely costs some intent accuracy and reply quality — measured, not assumed. The judge still runs on the same model as the drafter, so self-preference bias remains and is bounded only by the human agreement check in stage 7 |
| 18 | Call Groq through its official client, with a raw-HTTP fallback | The standard-library path returned `403 error code 1010` — Cloudflare rejecting urllib's default user-agent before the request reached the API. The failure is indistinguishable from a bad key at a glance, so the error message now names the real cause. The SDK is tried first and raw HTTP remains as a no-dependency fallback with a hand-set user-agent | One more dependency in `make setup`, ~5 seconds of install time |
| 19 | Settled on `openai/gpt-oss-120b` after querying the provider for what the key can actually reach | Three model retirements in one afternoon across two providers made published model names unreliable, so `scripts/check_models.py` asks the API instead. Of the chat-capable models available, this is the largest instruction-following one, and ~570 calls fits inside Groq's 1,000/day free quota. `reasoning_effort=low` keeps reasoning tokens out of the JSON the classifier has to parse | An open model rather than a frontier one; whatever accuracy that costs is measured, not assumed |
| 20 | The judge runs on a different model (`qwen/qwen3.8-27b`) from the drafter (`openai/gpt-oss-120b`) | Forced by a quota wall — Groq caps tokens per day *per model*, and the agent stage consumed 199,917 of 200,000 — but it is the better design anyway: a judge from another family cannot exhibit self-preference for its own output, which was an admitted weakness of the earlier same-model setup. It does not make the judge unbiased, only differently biased; the human agreement check remains what bounds it | Two model dependencies instead of one, and judge scores are not comparable to a run judged by the other model |
| 21 | Results are written to disk after each system, not once at the end | The first full run reached the judging stage with all 196 agent outputs computed, hit a token cap, raised, and wrote nothing. The expensive work survived only because of the response cache. Incremental writes mean a partial run yields partial results | A results file may describe fewer than three systems, so downstream readers must not assume all keys exist |
