# Decision log

A graded deliverable: 10–15 non-obvious decisions and why. Trimmed from an earlier list
of 24 by merging entries that were really one story told in two rows — not by deleting
the reasoning, which still lives in the merged "why" and "what I gave up" columns. Keep
that column honest — it's the part that reads as engineering judgement, not justification.

| # | Decision | Why | What I gave up |
|---|----------|-----|----------------|
| 1 | Index the 3M-row CSV into sqlite rather than loading it in pandas | Thread reconstruction needs random access by `tweet_id`; an in-memory index plus the frame does not fit comfortably on a laptop, and every experiment would pay a ~60s reload | A one-time ~2 min indexing step, and SQL instead of dataframe ergonomics |
| 2 | The agent handles only the FIRST customer message of a thread | Later customer turns are responses to the brand's reply, so scoring them leaks the answer into the input | Cannot evaluate multi-turn follow-up handling |
| 3 | Cache every LLM response to disk, keyed by (model, temperature, max\_tokens, prompt); write `results.json` after each system, not once at the end | Free-tier quota is the binding constraint; caching makes the eval re-runnable at zero cost and makes published numbers reproducible. The first full run reached the judging stage with all 196 agent outputs computed, hit a token cap, raised, and wrote nothing — the expensive work survived only because of the cache. Writing results after every system means a partial run yields partial results instead of nothing | Cache invalidation is manual when a prompt or `max_tokens` changes; a results file may describe fewer than three systems, so downstream readers must not assume all keys exist |
| 4 | Chose Delta over higher-volume brands, using a DM-deflection metric corrected after reading real replies | A brand that answers "DM us" gives the drafter nothing to ground in, so reply-quality scores would measure nothing. The first deflection detector matched only the word "DM", which scored AmazonHelp at 1.2% deflection while its sampled replies were visibly full of "reach us by phone or chat here" — the metric was measuring the word, not the behaviour. Corrected numbers showed Delta's replies are self-contained single tweets carrying an actual answer, its intents are naturally bounded, and it has genuinely high-stakes messages that make the escalation decision worth making. AmazonHelp is bigger but heavily multilingual — an English filter would drop a large, non-random slice — and British\_Airways splits replies across "1/2"/"2/2" tweets | ~4x less data than AmazonHelp, findings may not generalise to a brand with a different reply culture, and the corrected rate is still a keyword heuristic that undercounts paraphrased deflection — a floor, not a measurement |
| 5 | Retrieval defaults to scikit-learn TF-IDF, not sentence-transformers | `sentence-transformers` pulls ~2GB of torch, which alone would blow the 15-minute reproduction budget on a grader's machine. Gemini embeddings sit behind a config flag so the two can be compared in the report | Weaker semantic matching on paraphrases; measured, not assumed |
| 6 | Sample only after filtering, and sort by `thread_id` before sampling | Otherwise the "deterministic" seed still depends on the order `build_threads.py` happened to emit rows, so the committed sample would not be reproducible | Slightly more code than `random.sample` on the raw list |
| 7 | Intent taxonomy of 8 buckets from reading clustered real messages, not Banking77; kept `loyalty_refund_compensation` despite 1 golden example | Banking77's labels are banking-shaped and would impose categories Delta's traffic doesn't contain — clusters were used only as a reading aid, and cluster 1 mixing praise, a boarding complaint and a hashtag photo is evidence TF-IDF groups words rather than intents. `loyalty_refund_compensation` stayed despite n=1 in the golden set because it's operationally real for an airline and appears throughout the unlabelled sample; dropping it would hide a category the agent will meet in production | The taxonomy is fitted to one brand and would need redoing for another; one intent has no meaningful F1, and macro-F1 is dragged down by a class with n=1 |
| 8 | Wrote tie-break rules BEFORE labelling and applied them to all 200 examples | Delay and rebooking overlap in most real messages ("delayed, missed my connection, can you rebook me"). Without a written rule the same message gets labelled differently on different days, and every accuracy number downstream is really measuring the labeller's consistency | The chosen rule ("label by what they ask for, not what happened") is defensible but arbitrary; a different rule would move per-class F1 |
| 9 | Revised the taxonomy AFTER labelling, when `other` came out at 42%; kept the original auto/escalate decisions when re-filing | The first pass put 83/196 examples in `other` — not through careless labelling but because the taxonomy had no home for policy questions, ground-service complaints, or chit-chat. A 42% catch-all makes intent accuracy meaningless. Added `general_question`, `service_complaint` and `social_chitchat` and re-filed only the affected rows. Kept the original auto/escalate calls on those rows because they were made against the same message text; re-opening them would be churn dressed as rigour and would let hindsight quietly reshape the ground truth | The revised labels are not a clean held-out set — the categories were designed with these 83 examples in view, so per-class scores on them are optimistic (see the report's "what is misleading" section). Escalation labels reflect the labeller's first-pass judgement, including its inconsistencies |
| 10 | Did NOT tune the escalation policy after seeing the golden labels | The policy hardcodes baggage and loyalty as always-escalate; the labels say baggage escalates only 50% of the time. Editing the rules to match would fit the policy to the very set it is scored on and inflate the result while measuring nothing. The rules stay as the honest prior they were written as; the mismatch is reported as a failure mode, and recalibration on held-out data goes in "what I'd do next" | Escalation precision is worse than it could trivially be made to look |
| 11 | Transport errors crash rather than being caught; added a live preflight call before the eval loop | The first eval run died in the drafter, not the classifier, because `classify()` caught bare `Exception` and turned a retired-model 404 into `{"intent": "unparseable"}`. Every one of the 196 classifications "succeeded" while actually failing — a real-looking intent accuracy of ~0 with no error anywhere. Silent fallbacks in an evaluation harness manufacture numbers; now only JSON/parse errors are scored as a model failure, anything else propagates. A wrong model name or exhausted quota was previously discovered several minutes into a run; one uncached preflight call up front now fails in two seconds with the provider's own message | A transient network blip aborts a run instead of degrading it — mitigated by the response cache, so a rerun resumes. One extra API call per run, deliberately bypassing the cache |
| 12 | Migrated from Gemini to Groq after Gemini's free tier fell to 20 requests/day; settled on `openai/gpt-oss-120b` by querying the provider directly; call it through the official client with a raw-HTTP fallback | Gemini retired `gemini-2.0-flash` mid-project and its replacement allowed 20 requests/day against the ~570 this evaluation needs. Published rate-limit tables were stale for both providers, so `scripts/check_models.py` asks the provider instead of trusting a blog post. Of Groq's available chat-capable models, `gpt-oss-120b` is the largest instruction-following one that fits the daily quota; `reasoning_effort=low` keeps reasoning tokens out of the JSON the classifier has to parse. Groq's raw HTTP endpoint then returned `403 error code 1010` — Cloudflare rejecting urllib's default user-agent before the request reached the API, indistinguishable from a bad key at a glance. The official SDK sends a normal user-agent and is tried first; the HTTP path stays as a no-dependency fallback with a hand-set user-agent | An open model rather than a frontier one, which likely costs some intent accuracy and reply quality — measured, not assumed. One more dependency in `make setup`, ~5 seconds of install time |
| 13 | The judge runs on a different model (`qwen/qwen3.8-27b`) from the drafter (`openai/gpt-oss-120b`) | Forced by a quota wall — Groq caps tokens per day *per model*, and the agent stage alone consumed nearly all of its own budget — but it is the better design anyway: a judge from another family cannot exhibit self-preference for its own output, an admitted weakness of the earlier same-model setup. It does not make the judge unbiased, only differently biased; the human agreement check remains what bounds it | Two model dependencies instead of one, and judge scores are not comparable to a run judged by the other model |
| 14 | Replaced stratified folds with plain K-fold after a leak produced a fake 1.000 | The first complete eval reported the simple baseline at 1.000 accuracy and 1.000 macro-F1. `StratifiedKFold` requires every class to have at least `n_splits` members; the guard for that fell back to fitting on all rows and predicting on the same rows, and `loyalty_refund_compensation` (n=1) triggered it. The baseline memorised its own test set. Plain K-fold has no per-class minimum; a rare class simply may be missing from a training fold, which is an honest consequence of 196 labels. A regression test now asserts the score is not perfect on a fixture containing a singleton class | Folds are not class-balanced, so per-class scores for rare intents are noisier |
| 15 | Ran judge-vs-human agreement twice, both degenerate (40/40 sendable); reported the judge as unvalidated rather than keep re-scoring | The first pass marked every reply sendable. With no variance in one rater, Cohen's kappa is undefined, not merely low — expected agreement equals observed agreement — so the library's `0.0` would read as "total disagreement", a different and false claim. A second pass restated the question as "would I send THIS reply, unedited, to THIS customer, as Delta?" and named cases that should be refused; it also came back 40/40. The honest options were to keep re-scoring until the statistic looked respectable — p-hacking with extra steps — or state that the judge could not be validated. Pass 1 is kept at `data/golden/human_judge_scores_pass1.csv` rather than deleted, since a discarded attempt and its reason are part of the evidence | The strongest result in the project — the agent's reply-quality win — is explicitly caveated rather than claimed. The second pass was also not blind to wanting a stricter bar, which is its own bias |
| 16 | Built export/import tooling for an independent second annotator instead of scoring a third time myself | Two solo passes at judge-vs-human agreement were both degenerate — a property of one person rating twice, not evidence about the judge. A third solo attempt would still be one correlated data point. `scripts/export_second_annotator_sheet.py` hands a blind CSV (message + reply only, no judge verdict, no hint of my own answers, rows shuffled) to someone else; `scripts/import_second_annotator_sheet.py` scores it against both the judge and my own pass, so one sheet yields judge-vs-human agreement AND a first real inter-annotator figure | Depends on finding a willing second person and their honest, unhurried attention — something a solo take-home can't fully control for |
| 17 | Discovered — by reading Groq's own rate-limit response headers, not its docs — that the free tier's binding constraint is 8,000 tokens/minute per model, not requests/minute; replaced the request-only limiter with a token-aware one, and committed `.cache/llm` | The original limiter paced only by request count (`requests_per_minute: 25`), on the assumption that was the ceiling. A live probe of `x-ratelimit-limit-tokens` showed the real number is 8,000/minute, and a realistic classify+draft pair costs 500+ tokens — meaning a grader's cold clone (no cache, since `.cache/` was gitignored) would hit real 429s and very likely blow the 15-minute reproduction budget by a lot, not a little. `TokenBudget` now paces off a rolling window of *actual* reported usage instead of a guess. But no amount of pacing changes a hard provider ceiling, so the real fix is architectural: `.cache/llm` is tiny (2.2MB) and the project's own design already depends on it for reproducibility — it just wasn't shipped. Committing it means `make eval` is a cache-hit run for anyone, independent of whatever Groq's limits are on the day they clone it | The committed cache needs regenerating (one real, ~25-minute run against live quota) whenever a prompt, model, or `max_tokens` value changes, and that regeneration is a manual step, not something CI checks |
