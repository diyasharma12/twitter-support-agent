# Delta support agent — Hiver SDE Intern take-home

An AI customer-support agent for **Delta Air Lines**, built from the Kaggle *Customer
Support on Twitter* dataset, plus the evaluation needed to decide whether it can be
trusted. Every number below is generated by the pipeline into `reports/`, not typed by
hand.

**The one-line result: the agent appears to write better replies than either baseline and
makes a clearly worse routing decision than the trivial one.** It escalates 16% of the
messages a human said needed a human — a blocking defect, and diagnosing it is the most
useful thing in this repo. "Appears" is load-bearing: the reply-quality comparison rests
on an LLM judge I was **unable to validate against a human**, and that failure is
documented rather than papered over.

See also [`ARCHITECTURE.md`](ARCHITECTURE.md) for how it works and
[`DECISIONS.md`](DECISIONS.md) for the 24 non-obvious decisions behind it.

## Reproduce the headline results (< 15 minutes)

```bash
make setup                       # venv + 7 dependencies, no torch
cp .env.example .env             # add GROQ_API_KEY (free, no card: console.groq.com/keys)
make eval                        # 196 examples × 3 systems, then the judge
make judge-check                 # you score 50 replies blind; reports judge-vs-human kappa
```

`data/sample/` and `data/golden/` are committed, so the 493MB Kaggle file is not needed.
All LLM responses are cached in `.cache/llm`, so a re-run costs no quota. To rebuild from
raw: put `twcs.csv` in `data/raw/`, then `make index && make profile && make threads && make sample`.

## Problem framing: what "good" means for Delta

Delta's support Twitter is not a queue of tickets. In a stratified sample of 196 real
threads, **32% is praise or chit-chat** with nothing to resolve, and the largest genuine
support category is complaints about *service* — hold times, rude staff at a gate, a
dirty cabin — not about flights. Any framing that treats this channel as "customers
raising issues" is wrong about a third of the time before it starts.

So "good" here has an unusual shape:

1. **Never auto-handle something that needed a person.** A wrongly-escalated easy message
   costs a few minutes of an agent's time. A wrongly auto-handled stranded customer, or
   one whose father just died, costs Delta a customer and possibly a news cycle. The
   costs are asymmetric by orders of magnitude, so recall on escalation matters far more
   than precision, and ties escalate.
2. **Never invent a promise.** A reply that says "we've refunded you" when nothing was
   refunded is worse than no reply. Drafts are grounded in retrieved precedent and the
   judge scores groundedness separately from tone for exactly this reason.
3. **Deflection is not resolution.** Delta's own replies are often "DM us your
   confirmation number". Reproducing that faithfully would score well against the
   reference and help nobody, which is why reply quality is judged on a rubric rather
   than by similarity to what Delta actually said.

### What I chose not to build

- **Multi-turn conversation.** Only the first customer message is handled. Later turns
  respond to Delta's reply, so scoring them leaks the answer into the input.
- **Fine-tuning.** 196 labels is far too few, and they are worth more as a test set.
- **Automatic sending.** Even an `auto` decision produces a draft for review.
- **Banking77.** Its labels are banking-shaped and would impose categories Delta's traffic
  does not contain.
- **Embedding retrieval by default.** ~2GB of PyTorch would break the 15-minute
  reproduction budget; TF-IDF's weakness is measured instead of hidden.

## The golden set: how it was sampled and labelled

**196 examples, hand-labelled by me**, each with an intent and an auto/escalate decision.

*Sampling.* Delta's 35,987 reconstructed threads were filtered (message ≥ 15 chars,
English-looking) and deterministically subsampled to 4,000. Only threads with a brand
reply are eligible, since reply quality cannot be scored without a reference. The pool
was then stratified across 10 TF-IDF clusters, proportional to cluster size but with a
**floor of 8 per cluster**, so small-but-real issue types survive into the evaluation — a
purely random 200 would be dominated by the two largest clusters and would flatter any
model that only learns those. The pool was frozen and committed *before* labelling
started, so no inconvenient example could be quietly dropped.

*Labelling.* Tie-break rules were written **before** labelling and applied to all 196
(see `intents.py`): label by what the customer is asking for rather than what happened to
them; money or miles requested makes it `loyalty_refund_compensation` whatever caused it;
sarcasm with no request is `other`, not `praise`.

*The taxonomy changed because of the labelling.* The first pass put **42% of examples in
`other`** — not through careless labelling but because the initial 8 intents had no home
for policy questions ("do I get priority boarding with the Delta card?"), ground-service
complaints, or chit-chat. Adding `general_question`, `service_complaint` and
`social_chitchat` and re-filing only the affected rows took `other` from 42% to 5.1%.
This is reported rather than hidden because it is the main reason to hand-label at all:
labelling is how you discover your categories are wrong.

## Results

Full generated tables in [`reports/tables.md`](reports/tables.md). n = 196.
Agent: `openai/gpt-oss-120b`. Judge: `qwen/qwen3.8-27b` (deliberately a different family —
see below).

| system | intent acc | 95% CI | macro F1 | esc. recall | esc. precision | judged sendable |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| trivial | 0.245 | ±0.060 | 0.036 | 1.000 | 0.393 | 0.475 |
| simple | 0.357 | ±0.067 | 0.248 | 1.000 | 0.393 | 0.350 |
| **agent** | **0.490** | ±0.070 | **0.408** | **0.156** | 0.500 | **0.800** |

**Baselines.** *Trivial* = always the majority intent (`praise`), always escalate, one
fixed template. *Simple* = TF-IDF + logistic regression for intent (scored out-of-fold
with 5-fold CV, since it is the only system that learns from the labels),
nearest-neighbour historical reply, same escalation rules.

**Reading this honestly.** The agent beats the simple baseline on intent by 13.3 points,
which is outside the confidence interval and therefore a real difference. On reply
quality the gap is large and consistent across all three judge dimensions — grounded 4.53
vs 3.73, tone 4.15 vs 2.65, resolution 4.08 vs 2.63. But on the metric that matters most
operationally, **both baselines beat the agent outright** by escalating everything. That
is not a subtle finding; a support lead reading this table would not ship the agent.

Note also that escalation *recall* of 1.000 for the baselines is worthless on its own —
their auto-rate is 0.000, so they are doing no work. The agent's 0.878 auto-rate is the
value it offers, and 65 missed escalations is the price it currently charges for it.

## Failure analysis

### 1. Self-reported confidence is not a usable signal (root cause of the escalation failure)

The escalation policy has a rule: escalate when classifier confidence < 0.6. It **never
fired once**. Across 196 messages the model's self-reported confidence had a minimum of
**0.78**, a median of **0.97**, and nothing below 0.6. So 172 of 196 messages fell through
every rule to the `routine` default, which auto-handles.

*Hypothesis:* an LLM asked to emit a confidence number in JSON produces a stylistic
artefact, not a calibrated probability. It has no access to its own uncertainty and
outputs what a confident answer looks like. Any gate built on that number is decorative.

### 2. The routing decision ignores the draft, which already knows the answer

The most striking result in the repo. Of the 65 missed escalations, **51 (78%) have a
draft that asks the customer for a confirmation number or booking details** — the drafter
correctly recognised the issue needs private account data while the policy, looking only
at intent and a regex over the raw message, did not.

> **C:** "I have a question about a reservation that says I'm on the Standby List. Why don't I have a seat?"
> **Decision:** auto — "routine 'general_question' with confidence 0.97"
> **Draft:** "You're on standby for a seat assignment… Please DM us your confirmation number…"

The agent's two halves disagree, and the half that is right is ignored. As a diagnostic,
adding a single rule — *if the draft requests private details, escalate* — would move
recall from **0.156 to 0.818** while precision moves 0.500 → 0.543.

**This rule is deliberately not implemented.** It was derived from the golden set, so
implementing it and reporting 0.818 would be fitting the policy to its own test set. It
belongs in "next week", validated on held-out labels.

### 3. Adjacent intents collapse into each other

The three largest confusion pairs are `inflight_experience` → `delay_cancellation` (6),
`service_complaint` → `other` (6), and `inflight_experience` → `service_complaint` (5),
with `rebooking_change` → `service_complaint` (5) close behind. Per-class F1 for
`rebooking_change` is 0.31 and for `service_complaint` 0.40.

*Hypothesis:* real messages contain all three at once ("delayed, then the crew was rude,
now I need rebooking"). My tie-break rule resolves this by what the customer *asks for*,
but the prompt gives the model the intent definitions without the tie-break rules, so it
classifies by the most salient topic mentioned. The fix is prompt-level, not model-level.

### 4. `other` is unlearnable as specified

Precision 0.08, recall 0.10 on n=10. A category defined by exclusion has no positive
signal for a classifier to latch onto, and after the taxonomy revision the remaining
`other` rows have nothing in common except that they fit nowhere.

### 5. `loyalty_refund_compensation` has one example and scores 0.00 on everything

Not a model failure — a measurement failure. One example cannot produce a meaningful
per-class score, and its 0.00 drags macro-F1 down for reasons unrelated to model quality.
It was kept in the taxonomy because it is operationally real for an airline and appears
throughout the unlabelled sample; dropping it would hide a category the agent will meet
in production.

*Also worth noting:* not every "miss" is a model error. The agent auto-answered "Can a
six-year-old traveling with a parent fly without a government ID?" correctly and
completely, and I had labelled it escalate. Some disagreements are my labels being
conservative, which is itself a limit on how precisely any of these numbers can be read.

## What is misleading about my headline number?

The headline is *intent accuracy 0.490, judged sendable 0.800*. Both mislead, in six ways.

1. **0.490 on 11 classes sounds respectable and is barely a coin flip on the classes that
   matter.** It is buoyed by `praise` (n=48, F1 0.79), the easiest and least
   operationally interesting class. The classes a support team cares about score far
   worse: `rebooking_change` 0.31, `delay_cancellation` 0.31, `service_complaint` 0.40.
2. **n=196 gives ±0.07 at 95%.** The agent-vs-simple gap survives that; almost none of
   the per-class differences do. Any statement of the form "the agent is better at X than
   at Y" in this report is under-powered.
3. **Three of the eleven intents were defined after reading examples that are in the
   golden set.** `general_question`, `service_complaint` and `social_chitchat` were
   created because 42% of the first pass landed in `other`. Their per-class scores are
   optimistic by an unknown amount.
4. **The ground truth is one person, once.** There is no second annotator and therefore no
   inter-annotator agreement, so "accuracy" means "agreement with my labels on one
   afternoon", including my inconsistencies. This is the single largest unquantified
   error term in the report.
5. **"80% sendable" is an unvalidated judge score — see the section below.** Only 40 of
   196 replies were judged, and the attempt to validate the judge against a human failed
   to produce a usable statistic. This number should be read as "a model's opinion",
   not as a measurement of reply quality.
6. **Replies are drafted at temperature 0.3 and evaluated once.** There is no variance
   estimate across runs, so reply-quality differences of a few points should not be
   treated as stable.

And the number that is *not* misleading, which is the one that should decide anything:
**65 missed escalations out of 77.**

## Can the judge be trusted? Not on this evidence.

The assignment asks for evidence that the LLM judge agrees with a human. I ran that check
twice and **it failed both times, in the same way**: I marked all 40 replies sendable on
the first pass, and all 40 again on a second pass run with an explicitly stricter bar.

When one rater gives the same answer to every item, Cohen's kappa is not low — it is
**undefined**. Expected agreement equals observed agreement, so the correction for chance
divides out and the statistic carries no information. The library returns `0.0`, which
reads like "total disagreement" and would be a false claim in the other direction, so
`reports/judge_agreement.json` reports `null` with an explicit `degenerate: true` flag
rather than a misleading number.

Raw agreement was 0.80, but that is simply the judge's own sendable rate restated: if one
rater always says yes, raw agreement *is* the other rater's yes-rate, and it would have
been 0.80 for any judge with that rate, including a judge that answered at random with
the right base rate.

**What this means for the rest of the report.** Every reply-quality number — sendable
0.800, grounded 4.53, tone 4.15, resolution 4.08, and the agent's apparent win over both
baselines on reply quality — rests on an unvalidated judge. The intent and escalation
numbers are unaffected, because those are scored against hand labels, not against a
model.

**Why it failed, honestly.** Reviewing my own answers against the drafts, the bar I
applied was "is this a reasonable customer-support reply?" rather than "would I send this
to *this* customer, unedited, as Delta?" Under the second question at least a few should
have been refused — a generic "please DM your confirmation number" sent to a customer who
has been on hold for two hours, or to someone describing a repeated pattern of late
flights, is not sendable. Both passes were also run by the same person within hours of
each other, which is not a sound annotation design regardless of the bar used.

The first pass is kept at `data/golden/human_judge_scores_pass1.csv` alongside the second
rather than deleted, since a discarded attempt and its reason are part of the evidence.

Fixing this properly needs a second annotator, which is the first item in the next-week
list below.

## What I'd do with one more week

1. **Replace self-reported confidence with something calibrated** — token-level logprobs
   where the provider exposes them, or disagreement between two cheap classifiers — and
   check calibration against the golden labels before trusting it as a gate.
2. **Make the draft an input to the routing decision** (failure mode 2), validated on a
   fresh held-out label set rather than the one that suggested it.
3. **Get a second annotator** — for two things, not one. First to validate the judge,
   since my own two attempts both produced a degenerate all-yes pass and the judge is
   currently unvalidated. Second to score 50 golden examples independently so the ground
   truth itself has an inter-annotator agreement figure. Without both, every quality
   figure here has an unmeasured floor.
4. **Put the tie-break rules into the classifier prompt** and re-measure the adjacent-intent
   confusions — the cheapest experiment available, and failure mode 3 predicts it helps.
5. **Build a second golden slice stratified by *decision* rather than intent**, so the
   escalation metric rests on more than 77 positives.
6. **Add a model fallback chain and a startup availability check.** Three model
   retirements across two providers happened during a single day of building this; a
   production system pinning one hosted model name is one deprecation away from an outage.
7. **Measure cost and latency per handled message**, which no number here currently
   captures, and which decides whether any of this is worth deploying.
